SOC HANDOFF · MISSION CONTROL

What the SOC needs

The SOC needs precise observations, reliable machine data, normalized context, detections, findings, and defensible analyst judgment. Reporting something suspicious is not the same as declaring an incident. A useful report states what was observed, what was verified, and what remains unknown so analysts can investigate without inheriting the reporter’s assumptions.

Concept foundation: vulnerability → threat → exploit → risk
VulnerabilityA weakness, condition, or exposure that could be used by a threat. A vulnerability does not mean harm has already occurred; it describes an opportunity for harm if a capable threat can reach and exploit it. Examples in the source include an email server without spam filtering, an unpatched computer, and a weak password on a critical system.
ThreatA person, group, event, or condition with the capability to take advantage of a vulnerability and cause harm. A threat can exist even when no exploitation has occurred; examples include a phisher, ransomware gang, or military cyber organization.
ExploitThe technique, action, or mechanism used to take advantage of a vulnerability. Think of the vulnerability as the weakness and the exploit as the way the weakness is actually used; examples include a successful phishing email, a trojan hidden in media, or a virus manipulating logs.
RiskThe potential for loss or harm when a valuable asset is exposed to a threat through a vulnerability. Risk asks what could happen and why it matters to the organization; natural risks also exist. Examples include ransomware lockout and loss of trust in manipulated logs.

Cybersecurity Awareness — complete concept map

Security Awareness & terminology

Security awareness; responsibility; vulnerability, threat, exploit, risk; weaknesses can include technical states and human awareness; threats are possible actors/actions; exploits manipulate vulnerabilities; risk can include cyber and natural conditions.

Social engineering & phishing

CEO/invoice-style deception; human-needs framing; self-actualization, esteem, belonging, safety, physiological needs; quid pro quo, urgency, authority, bandwagoning; sender verification; “does it feel right?”; spelling/cadence; unverified phone numbers; impersonal salutations; “what is the ask?”; urgent deadlines; improper addresses; suspicious/typosquatted URLs; authority cues; report phishing regardless of interaction; use organizational criteria/process; if interaction occurred, promptly contact IT/IT Security/management as policy requires.

Recognizing malware

Malware is complex; myths about “safe” websites, antimalware infallibility, and being too unimportant to target; possible indicators include sudden popups/adware, startup command-line windows, periodic versus massive sudden slowdowns, browser behavior changes, antimalware alerts, and firewall-change requests; involve IT when context is uncertain.

Suspicious activities & practice

Security spans physical and information assets. Report what seems out of place using organizational mechanisms. Examples include tailgating, strange devices, and unusual behavior. Practice scenarios reinforce never sharing passwords/accounts, helping coworkers obtain legitimate access instead, refusing unauthenticated facility entry while offering an approved help path, and reporting reasonable concerns. Information security, physical security, and cybersecurity work together; security is everyone's job.

Enterprise Security — complete concept map

ES purpose, APTs & frameworks

ES is a SIEM-oriented security application on Splunk. It helps detect, investigate, and respond; supports security posture/compliance context; integrates with SOAR, Threat Intelligence Management, and UEBA. APTs are prolonged targeted adversary activity. Frameworks include Cyber Kill Chain, MITRE ATT&CK, CIS Controls, NIST, and PCI.

Interface & access

Access ES from Splunk Enterprise Apps/applications; ES Home links key tools. Mission Control, Analytics dashboards, Security Content, Configure, and Search serve different functions. Content Management exposes detection/security objects; administrative changes are role-limited. Roles include ess_user, ess_analyst, and ess_admin with progressively broader capabilities.

Data, CIM, models & KV Store

Forwarders gather diverse machine data; indexers store events. CIM normalizes fields; data models organize data; acceleration speeds dependent searches/dashboards/reports/alerts. Data-model editing is administrative. KV Store maintains workflow/status/comments/lookups/assets/identities/threat-intel collections and is important to ES operation.

Detections, findings & risk

Predefined/custom detections can run scheduled or real-time. Findings (formerly notable events) can reach the Analyst Queue/notable index; intermediate findings (formerly risk events) accumulate in the risk index. Security Posture and Risk Analysis help analysts view findings/risk. Asset/identity priority can affect urgency. Findings can be owned, status-updated, annotated, and grouped into investigations.

Search examples

Search can explore index=notable and refine by fields/source. Data-model searching may use from or tstats. tstats supports efficient accelerated-model analysis with count/FROM/WHERE/BY concepts; object-qualified fields matter. summariesonly can restrict work to accelerated summaries, improving speed while excluding data outside the summary range.

SOC roles & workflows

Tier 1, Tier 2, SOC Manager, Threat Hunter, Detection Engineer, and Incident Responder have different responsibilities. Workflows include Triage, Investigation, Hunting, Detection, and Threat Intelligence. The Security Use Cases Library is presented as a resource for applying ES features to practical detection, investigation, and response use cases.
