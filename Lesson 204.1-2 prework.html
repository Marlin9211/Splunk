Prework • Orientation

A routine morning becomes a security story.

You are the newest Security Readiness Associate at Meridian Exchange, a regional organization that coordinates time-sensitive services across offices, vendors, and remote teams. At 8:17 a.m., an ordinary workday begins to change: an employee reports a strange account message, a controlled door is held for an unfamiliar visitor, and the security team begins seeing technical activity worth reviewing. Your prework follows that same morning from the employee’s first uneasy feeling to the analyst’s view inside Splunk Enterprise Security.

Your mission

Follow Meridian Exchange’s morning security story in chronological order. First recognize the language employees and analysts use; then interpret the human warning signs; finally connect the technical signals to the introductory Splunk ES workflow.

Story path

Reporter · Remember covers the first observations and shared vocabulary. Translator · Understand follows the same case into relationships, ES workflow, and the human-to-analyst bridge. Each briefing advances the same morning rather than starting a new scenario.

Estimated time: about 40 minutes. This prework does not ask you to troubleshoot incidents or operate a production SIEM. It prepares you for later Apply and Analyze work.

Complete Coverage • Source Atlas

Everything the two lessons contribute

This atlas consolidates the complete meaningful instructional content of both uploaded lessons before you practice it. Use the tabs to review each source strand. The activities that follow deliberately reuse these concepts so they are not merely mentioned.

Cybersecurity Awareness — foundation

Security awareness: cybersecurity is everyone’s responsibility. Individual decisions can reduce or increase organizational risk.

Core terminology: vulnerability, threat, exploit, and risk. A vulnerability is a weakness; a threat is something capable of causing harm; an exploit uses a weakness; risk concerns the possibility and impact of harm.

Security terminology relationships: learners should distinguish the terms rather than treat them as interchangeable and understand how they combine in a real security situation.

Social engineering & phishing

Social engineering targets people and their motivations. The lesson connects manipulation with human needs and motivations, including safety, belonging, and self-actualization, and shows how attackers can use persuasion to influence behavior.

Phishing can use quid pro quo, urgency, authority, and other manipulation techniques. Learners are taught to inspect the entire message rather than react to one cue.

Email red flags: unexpected sender/domain, generic or unusual greeting, urgency, credential requests, suspicious links, questionable attachments, spelling/grammar or formatting problems, and requests that conflict with normal organizational practice.

Phishing response & reporting

Do not interact with a suspicious message simply because it appears urgent. Stop, inspect, verify through trusted channels, and follow the organization’s reporting process.

Reporting matters because security teams need the suspicious message and its context to investigate. Awareness therefore includes both recognition and appropriate reporting.

Recognizing malware

Malware is complicated and can affect users even when they believe they are careful. Security is not guaranteed merely because antimalware exists, because a user believes nobody is targeting them, or because they have previously avoided infection.

The lesson reinforces practical awareness of malicious software, risky links/attachments, software and device behavior, and the importance of organizational protections and user decisions working together.

Suspicious activities

Employees have a responsibility to notice and report suspicious situations rather than assume someone else will handle them. Examples in the lesson include suspicious physical or workplace behavior, unusual requests, people seeking access or information, and circumstances that do not fit normal expectations.

Awareness applies to both digital and physical situations. The learner should observe, avoid unsafe disclosure or access, and use the organization’s reporting path.

Practice scenarios from the awareness lesson

The lesson uses workplace decisions such as incident requests, account/access questions, password sharing, tailgating/access behavior, and suspicious situations. The common principle is to verify identity and authorization rather than relying on familiarity, urgency, politeness, or pressure.

The lesson closes with the idea that security is everyone’s job: employees, managers, IT, and security functions all contribute to the organization’s security posture.

Enterprise Security — SIEM foundation

Splunk Enterprise Security (ES) is introduced as a SIEM: a security-focused solution that brings together machine/security data so analysts can recognize, investigate, and respond to threats.

The course explains why organizations need this capability, what Splunk ES does, and how it fits with broader security operations rather than treating ES as a generic search tool.

Threat & incident framework

The Enterprise Security lesson introduces the anatomy of a security incident, kill chain methodology, advanced persistent threats (APTs), and ES security use cases. The purpose is to understand that attacks develop through stages and that security data can expose activity across those stages.

The course also introduces security frameworks/operations concepts to help learners understand how analysts organize security work and how ES supports that work.

Splunk ES roles

The lesson distinguishes ES responsibilities and access through roles such as User, Power, Admin, and Analyst. The point is that different users interact with Splunk/ES according to different responsibilities and permissions; the analyst role is centered on security investigation work.

Data in Splunk ES

The source-to-security-intelligence story includes gathering data, indexing events, searching data, looking for trouble, process summary, dashboards, and data models.

Machine/security data is collected from sources, stored as searchable events, and organized so analysts and security content can work with it. Accelerated data models improve access to normalized security information used by ES analytics.

Threat detection process

ES uses correlation searches/detections to look for defined security patterns. When conditions are met, ES can create notable events/findings for analyst attention. The conceptual flow is:

Security data → search/normalized data → detection/correlation search → suspicious activity → finding/notable event → analyst investigation → response.

The lesson also introduces exploring searches in Splunk ES so learners understand that detection logic is grounded in searchable event data.

Dashboards, risk & security intelligence

The course introduces ES dashboards including Security Posture, Incident Review, and Executive Summary, plus risk-based alerting. Dashboards provide different views of security information; risk-based alerting adds context around entities and activity instead of treating every signal as an unrelated event.

Working in ES

The lesson’s final portion connects the environment to analyst work: security content, detection/investigation workflow, threat intelligence, and analyst roles. It shows that ES is an analyst-facing security environment, while remaining introductory rather than a full SOC analyst operations course.

Its workflow framing includes Trigger Workflow, Investigation Workflow, Detection Workflow, Threat Intelligence Workflow, and the people/roles that support them.

The bridge between both lessons

The awareness lesson develops the human side of security: recognize manipulation or suspicious activity, protect information and access, and report concerns. The Enterprise Security lesson develops the introductory analyst/SIEM side: collect and search data, detect meaningful activity, surface findings, and support investigation.

Together they create one mental model: people notice and report; systems collect and correlate; analysts investigate; the organization responds.

Coverage rule used in this build: concepts from both sources are consolidated when they teach the same relationship, but no major instructional strand is intentionally omitted. The application stays at Remember and Understand because this is prework.

Reporter • Evidence Route 02

Data Route Strip: Follow the Security Signal

8:46 a.m. — The morning report has reached the SOC. Now follow one security signal as it moves from collected machine data toward analyst attention in Splunk Enterprise Security.

Activity brief Why this matters: Splunk ES does not jump directly from a device to an investigation. Different parts of the workflow collect, store, organize, evaluate, and present security information.

What to examine: Each route checkpoint describes what is happening to the signal at that moment. Focus on the function of the stage rather than one familiar word.

What to do: For each checkpoint, select the stage that best explains what happens next. Options are deliberately similar in length and their positions vary. Then choose Validate route.

If incorrect: You will receive a clue about the stage you selected, but the application will not reveal the correct answer. Reconsider the evidence and try again.

Success: Reconstruct the introductory source workflow from security data through analyst investigation while understanding why neighboring stages do not fit.
Case thread: A workstation associated with the earlier account-verification message is producing security telemetry. You are tracing how that evidence becomes something an analyst can review—not deciding whether the activity is malicious.
Reporter • Message Triage 03

Red Flag Markup: Suspicious Message Review

8:31 a.m. — Maya Chen has forwarded the account-verification message that started the morning’s concern. Instead of hunting for one “bad-looking” clue, review the message the way an awareness-trained employee should: examine context, persuasion, identity, destination, and requested action together.

Activity brief Why this matters: Phishing often works because individual details can look ordinary. The stronger judgment comes from recognizing how multiple cues combine—especially urgency, authority, credential requests, sender inconsistencies, and suspicious destinations.

What to examine: Review each evidence record from the message. Some details are suspicious; others are neutral or only become meaningful when combined with context.

What to do: For each record, choose the interpretation that best explains its security significance. Options are intentionally plausible and vary in position. Then choose Validate message review.

If incorrect: You will learn why your interpretation does not fit, but the correct choice will remain hidden until you reconsider the evidence.

Success: Distinguish a meaningful phishing indicator from a normal message feature without relying on obvious wording or answer length.

Reporter • Access Desk 04

Access Desk: Route the Security Request

9:12 a.m. — As the morning case develops, several Splunk Enterprise Security requests arrive at the readiness desk. Your job is not to solve the requests. Your job is to recognize which ES role or workspace is the appropriate starting point.

Activity brief Why this matters: Splunk ES separates responsibilities and workspaces. Analysts, administrators, and general ES users do not all perform the same work, and Mission Control, Security Content, Search, and broader analytics views serve different purposes.

What to examine: Each ticket contains a realistic work request. Identify the primary responsibility or destination described—not every tool that could possibly appear later in the workflow.

What to do: Review each ticket and select the best routing destination. The choices use legitimate ES roles/workspaces, their wording is intentionally comparable, and correct answers appear in different positions.

If incorrect: You will receive an explanation of why your selected destination is plausible but not the best fit. The correct answer stays hidden until you reconsider the ticket.

Success: Recognize who is responsible for the work and where that work begins without confusing investigation, configuration, content management, ad hoc search, and high-level security visibility.
Story connection: The suspicious message has been reported and the technical signal has entered ES. These tickets show how different people and ES views support the security program around that same workflow.

Translator • Comparison & Differentiation 04

Human Signal Conference: Compare What the Clues Actually Mean

10:18 a.m. — Meridian’s awareness team is preparing a five-minute staff debrief. Four reports arrived during the morning, and the team wants to prevent a common mistake: treating every unusual event as proof of the same security problem.

Activity brief Why this matters: Security awareness depends on interpreting clues carefully. Urgency, authority, credential requests, unusual device behavior, tailgating, and other warning signs can matter—but a clue is not automatically proof of compromise.

What to do: Open each employee report and compare the three interpretation notes written by the awareness team. Select the interpretation that best preserves what the evidence supports. The alternatives are intentionally similar in length and use realistic security reasoning.

If your interpretation is incorrect: The report remains open and you receive coaching about the misconception behind that choice. The strongest interpretation is not revealed.

When correct: You receive expert feedback explaining why your interpretation fits and why both competing interpretations do not fit as well.

Success: Distinguish warning signs, manipulation techniques, suspicious activity, and appropriate reporting without turning uncertainty into certainty.

Translator • Process Understanding 05

Threat Journey Room: Trace Framework → Detection → Analyst Meaning

10:37 a.m. — Meridian’s training team is preparing a wall-sized orientation map for new SOC analysts. The problem is not that the terms are missing; the problem is that new analysts often treat attack frameworks, detections, findings, data models, and threat intelligence as if they all describe the same layer of security work.

Activity brief Why this matters: Enterprise Security brings together attack-framework thinking, organized security data, detection logic, analyst-visible findings, and contextual intelligence. Understanding how these ideas relate prevents a learner from confusing an attack model with a detection, or a detection with the information an analyst eventually reviews.

What to do: Work through three investigation strips. For each strip, place the three evidence tiles into the conceptual order that best explains the relationship, then choose the explanation that most accurately describes why that order makes sense. This is a process-understanding activity, not an operational incident-response task.

What to focus on: Trace meaning across layers: how a framework describes behavior, how ES can organize/evaluate security information, and how resulting information becomes useful to an analyst.

Success: Reconstruct all three conceptual pathways and explain the distinction between the layers without treating them as interchangeable.
Cognitive work note: Do not ask, “What would I do next in a real incident?” Ask, “What does each layer represent, and how does its meaning connect to the next layer?”
Translator • Knowledge Synthesis 06

Shift Change Brief: Build the Security Story

11:02 a.m. — Meridian’s morning orientation is ending. Before the afternoon team arrives, the Readiness Lead needs a concise shift-change brief explaining how the two courses fit together. Your job is not to repeat definitions. It is to turn several source notes into one coherent security story.

Activity brief Why this matters: The awareness course explains how people recognize suspicious situations and make safer decisions. What to do: First, inspect the six notes in the shift packet. Cognitive work note: Strong synthesis keeps boundaries intact. Ask: “What does this layer contribute?” and then “How does that contribution connect to the larger security picture?”
